THE WALLED GARDEN

30‑minute pilot • mythic sci‑fi drama • projected as a Gaussian‑splat universe
Main Character: Steve
Presence: Grim
Theme: From fenced to unfenced
Visual: Point‑cloud / Gaussian splat
Logline

When Steve, a quiet maintenance worker in a vast botanical conservatory, begins seeing shimmering splat‑world overlays of parallel Earths, he encounters Grim — a heat‑bearing, mountain‑standing presence that exists across realities. As the walls of Steve’s inner “walled garden” begin to dissolve, he must decide whether to step beyond the boundaries of the life he thought he knew.

Tone & Format

Intimate, mythic, and visual. The pilot plays like a single unfolding vision: Gaussian‑splat particles forming and dissolving environments, with Steve as the only grounded human anchor. Grim is a recurring silhouette — hot inside, cold outside — always framed against impossible peaks.

Concept frame: Steve inside the walled garden, rendered as a Gaussian‑splat point‑cloud.

Main characters

Steve

34, quiet, observant, and slightly out of phase with his own life. He works maintenance at a sprawling botanical conservatory — a literal walled garden. He feels the space is beautiful but too curated, too bounded. His arc in the pilot: from enclosed to aware to destabilized to awakened.

Grim

A recurring presence that exists across parallel Earths. Not a villain, not a savior — more like a principle in human silhouette. Hot inside, cold outside. He appears as a point‑cloud figure standing on impossible peaks, always slightly distant, always watching. His function in the pilot is to mirror Steve’s unfenced potential.

Concept frame: Grim on the Maya‑like peaks — hot core, cold posture, rendered as layered splats.

Capture Requirements – Camera Count & Setup

How Many Cameras Are Required?

Gaussian‑splat filmmaking is flexible: you can capture with a single camera or a full multi‑camera rig depending on the fidelity you want.

Single‑Camera Capture (Minimum)

  • 1 camera
  • Walk around the subject or environment
  • Best for static scenes or environments
  • Lower cost, slower capture, more manual cleanup

Dual‑Camera Capture (Recommended Minimum for Characters)

  • 2 cameras mounted side‑by‑side or offset
  • Improves depth estimation and reduces occlusion gaps
  • Ideal for capturing Steve or Grim in motion

Multi‑Camera Array (High‑Fidelity)

  • 6–12 cameras arranged in a semicircle or full ring
  • Captures full volumetric detail in a single moment
  • Used for premium splat or NeRF character capture
  • Best for close‑ups, emotional beats, or complex motion

Environment Capture

For large spaces (gardens, peaks, corridors), a single roaming camera or two alternating cameras is sufficient. The key is coverage, not quantity.

How to Capture a Frame of Data

What a “Frame of Data” Means in Splat Filmmaking

A frame isn’t just a single video frame — it’s a bundle of information: image pixels, camera pose, depth cues, and metadata. Each frame becomes a data point the splat model uses to reconstruct the 3D world.

Step‑by‑Step Frame Capture Process

1. Position the Camera

  • Keep a consistent distance from the subject
  • Move smoothly (gimbal recommended)
  • Avoid sudden rotations or whip‑pans

2. Record High‑Resolution Video

  • 4K or 6K preferred
  • 24–30 fps is enough (higher fps not required)
  • Lock exposure and white balance

3. Maintain Overlap

Each frame must share visual overlap with the previous one so the AI can track features. Think of it like painting the scene with your camera.

4. Capture Multiple Angles

  • Walk around the subject
  • Capture high angles, low angles, and side passes
  • For characters, capture front, sides, and back

5. Extract Frames

After recording, the AI‑coordinator extracts frames at intervals (e.g., every 3rd or 5th frame) to create the training dataset.

6. Generate Camera Poses

Tools like COLMAP or Nerfstudio compute the exact position and orientation of the camera for each frame — this is essential for splat training.

7. Feed Into Training Pipeline

Each frame becomes a point in the optimization process:

  • Color
  • Opacity
  • Depth
  • Point position
  • Anisotropy (shape)

Result

The system reconstructs the scene as a dense cloud of Gaussian splats, ready for rendering, animation, and cinematic camera paths.
